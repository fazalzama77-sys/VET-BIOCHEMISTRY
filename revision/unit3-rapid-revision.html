01 Hormonal control of carbohydrate metabolism & regulation of blood sugar

Blood glucose is held within narrow limits by one hypoglycaemic hormone opposed by five hyperglycaemic ones. Sources of blood glucose: intestinal absorption, hepatic glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis. Losses: tissue uptake and oxidation, glycogenesis, lipogenesis, and — above the renal threshold — urinary loss.

Why ruminant values are low and why it matters: the ruminant absorbs almost no glucose from the gut and depends wholly on gluconeogenesis from propionate. Its "normal" 45–60 mg/dL is therefore not hypoglycaemia, and its margin for extra demand is thin — which is precisely why ketosis and pregnancy toxaemia are ruminant diseases and diabetes mellitus is rare in cattle.

02 Diabetes mellitus & hyperinsulinism

Diabetes mellitus — chronic hyperglycaemia from absolute or relative insulin deficiency. Type 1 (IDDM): β-cell destruction, absolute deficiency, ketosis-prone — the usual canine form. Type 2 (NIDDM): insulin resistance with islet amyloid deposition — the usual feline form, linked to obesity and inactivity. Secondary/type 3: dioestrus and progesterone/GH in the bitch, hyperadrenocorticism, glucocorticoid or megestrol therapy, pancreatitis, acromegaly in the cat.

  • Cardinal signs — the "3 P's plus": polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia with weight loss; later anorexia, vomiting, dehydration, acetone breath, Kussmaul respiration, cataract (dog — sorbitol/polyol pathway), plantigrade stance (cat — diabetic neuropathy), poor wound healing and recurrent infection, hepatic lipidosis.
  • Biochemical picture: persistent fasting hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, ketonaemia/ketonuria, metabolic acidosis with increased anion gap, hypercholesterolaemia and lipaemia, ↑ALP and ALT, dehydration with initial hyperkalaemia but total-body potassium depletion, and hyponatraemia (pseudo, from osmotic dilution).
Trap — the cat. Restraint alone can push a cat's glucose over 300 mg/dL by adrenaline release. Stress hyperglycaemia is transient and does not cause a raised fructosamine, so fructosamine (or glycated haemoglobin) is used to prove that hyperglycaemia has been present for weeks.

Hyperinsulinism (insulinoma) — a functional β-cell tumour, chiefly of the middle-aged to older dog (ferrets also). Autonomous insulin secretion causes fasting hypoglycaemia with weakness, ataxia, muscle tremor, collapse and seizures, classically relieved by feeding. Whipple's triad: neurological signs + documented hypoglycaemia + relief on giving glucose. Diagnosis rests on an inappropriately normal or high serum insulin measured on the same sample as a low glucose (raised insulin : glucose ratio). Compare with the other causes of hypoglycaemia: hepatic failure and portosystemic shunt, hypoadrenocorticism, sepsis, neonatal and toy-breed hypoglycaemia, hunting-dog hypoglycaemia, xylitol ingestion, glycogen storage disease, and delay in separating serum (glycolysis in vitro).

The examiner's favourite comparison: ketosis is a disease of the lactating cow with an outflow of glucose into milk; pregnancy toxaemia is a disease of the pregnant ewe with an outflow into foetuses; baby-pig hypoglycaemia is a disease of absent reserve and absent intake. The first two are ketotic, the third is not — because ketosis requires fat to burn, and the newborn piglet has almost none (≈1–2 % body fat and no brown adipose tissue).
Trap: ketosis has a primary form (pure negative energy balance) and a far commoner secondary form driven by another disease that stops the cow eating — displaced abomasum, traumatic reticuloperitonitis, metritis, mastitis, lameness. Never treat the ketones without hunting for the primary lesion.

  • Hypoalbuminaemia — reduced synthesis (chronic liver disease, malnutrition, chronic inflammation), increased loss (protein-losing nephropathy — glomerulonephritis, amyloidosis; protein-losing enteropathy; burns; severe haemorrhage), or sequestration. Consequences: oedema, ascites and effusions once albumin falls below ≈1.5 g/dL — the direct application of oncotic pressure and the Donnan effect from Unit 1.
  • Hyperglobulinaemia — polyclonal (a broad-based γ peak: chronic infection, ehrlichiosis, FIP, trypanosomiasis, dirofilariasis, chronic dermatitis, liver disease) or monoclonal (a tall narrow spike: multiple myeloma, plasmacytoma, lymphoma, monoclonal gammopathy).
  • A : G ratio falls in almost every chronic disease — either because albumin falls, globulin rises, or both. A total protein that looks normal can hide a severe dysproteinaemia, which is why the ratio, not the total, is the diagnostic figure.
  • Failure of passive transfer in the calf, foal, lamb and kid — inadequate colostral IgG gives a low total protein and low γ fraction in the first days of life; screened by total protein by refractometer, zinc sulphate turbidity, glutaraldehyde coagulation or GGT.

06 Acute-phase proteins (APPs)

Definition: plasma proteins whose concentration changes by at least 25 % within 24–48 hours of tissue injury, infection, inflammation, surgery or neoplasia, driven by IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α acting on the hepatocyte. They are non-specific but exquisitely sensitive early markers, and they rise long before leucocyte changes appear.

Two species syndromes to name: ① Equine hyperlipaemia — obese ponies, miniature horses and donkeys, especially pregnant or lactating mares under stress or anorexia; massive VLDL/TG rise, opaque plasma, fatty infiltration of liver and kidney, high mortality. ② Feline hepatic lipidosis — the anorectic obese cat mobilises fat faster than its liver can export VLDL. Trap: dogs and cats are "HDL animals" — HDL, not LDL, is the predominant lipoprotein — so spontaneous atherosclerosis is rare. A hypercholesterolaemic dog is telling you about its thyroid, adrenal, kidney or pancreas, not about its coronary arteries.

08 Clinical enzymology

Functional plasma enzymes are secreted into plasma to act there and rise in liver disease (coagulation factors, lipoprotein lipase, ceruloplasmin, pseudocholinesterase — these fall in hepatic failure). Non-functional plasma enzymes have no role in plasma; they leak from damaged cells and their rise indicates cell injury or induced synthesis — these are the diagnostic enzymes.

12 Disturbances of acid–base balance and their diagnosis

Everything follows from Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = 6.1 + log (HCO₃⁻ / 0.03 × pCO₂), normally 6.1 + log 20 = 7.4. HCO₃⁻ is the metabolic (renal) component; pCO₂ is the respiratory (pulmonary) component. Name the primary disorder by asking which one moved in the same direction as the pH change.

Anion gap = (Na⁺ + K⁺) − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), normally ≈ 12–25 mEq/L. A high anion gap means unmeasured acid has been added — ketoacids, lactate, uraemic acids, ethylene-glycol metabolites. A normal (hyperchloraemic) anion gap means bicarbonate has simply been lost — diarrhoea, renal tubular acidosis. This one calculation separates the two families of metabolic acidosis.

How to read a blood-gas report in four steps: ① look at pH — acidaemia or alkalaemia; ② look at HCO₃⁻ and pCO₂ and see which one explains the pH — that is the primary disorder; ③ check whether the other has moved in the same direction — that is compensation (compensation never overshoots to normal); ④ if both move in opposite directions, or the compensation is inappropriate, call it a mixed disorder.

13 Biochemistry of digestive disorders

  • Maldigestion vs malabsorption. Maldigestion = failure to break food down — chiefly exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI), common in the German Shepherd: steatorrhoea, voluminous pale faeces, ravenous appetite with weight loss; confirmed by a low serum TLI (trypsin-like immunoreactivity), with low cobalamin and high folate. Malabsorption = failure of the mucosa to take up the products — infiltrative bowel disease, villous atrophy, parasitism, lymphangiectasia; assessed by D-xylose absorption, faecal fat, cobalamin and folate.
  • Protein-losing enteropathy — loss of albumin and globulin into the gut (lymphangiectasia, chronic enteropathy, parasitism, Johne's disease) → panhypoproteinaemia, oedema, ascites. Contrast with protein-losing nephropathy, which loses albumin selectively.
  • Ruminal disorders — the biochemical trio: simple indigestion (rumen pH 6–7, poor motility); ruminal lactic acidosis (pH <5, ↑D- and L-lactate, systemic acidosis); ruminal alkalosis from urea overfeeding or putrefaction (pH >7.5–8, high ammonia). Rumen fluid examination — colour, odour, consistency, pH, methylene-blue reduction time, protozoal motility, sedimentation-flotation, chloride (>30 mEq/L suggests abomasal reflux) — is a favourite practical question.
  • Abomasal displacement and vomiting sequester HCl → hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria.
  • Diarrhoea — secretory (enterotoxigenic E. coli, cholera-like, mediated by cAMP), osmotic (lactose intolerance, overfeeding), inflammatory/exudative, and dysmotility. All lose water, sodium, potassium and bicarbonate → dehydration + metabolic acidosis.

14 Oxidative stress & shock

Oxidative stress = an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the antioxidant defence, in favour of the ROS, causing damage to lipid, protein and DNA.

O₂→Superoxide O₂•⁻SOD (Cu, Zn, Mn)→H₂O₂catalase (Fe) · glutathione peroxidase (Se)→H₂O
  • Sources of ROS: leakage from the electron transport chain, the respiratory burst of neutrophils (NADPH oxidase, myeloperoxidase), xanthine oxidase during ischaemia–reperfusion, cytochrome P450, transition metals via the Fenton and Haber–Weiss reactions, radiation, mycotoxins and xenobiotics.
  • Antioxidant defence — enzymatic: superoxide dismutase (Cu-Zn cytosolic, Mn mitochondrial), catalase, glutathione peroxidase (selenium), glutathione reductase (needs NADPH from the HMP shunt), thioredoxin. Non-enzymatic: vitamin E (α-tocopherol — the chain-breaking membrane antioxidant), vitamin C, β-carotene, glutathione, uric acid, taurine, ceruloplasmin, transferrin, albumin, zinc.
  • Consequences: lipid peroxidation (measured as MDA / TBARS), membrane and enzyme damage, DNA strand breaks, and activation of NF-κB with cytokine release.
  • Veterinary conditions in which oxidative stress is central: white muscle disease and mulberry heart disease (Se/vitamin E deficiency), yellow fat disease in cats, transition-cow disease and retained placenta, mastitis and metritis, ischaemia–reperfusion after gastric dilatation-volvulus and colic, onion, copper, paracetamol, nitrate and mycotoxin poisonings, heat stress in poultry, and ageing.

Shock — acute generalised failure of tissue perfusion. Whatever the type (hypovolaemic, distributive/septic, cardiogenic, obstructive), the biochemistry converges: inadequate O₂ delivery → anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acid accumulation → metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap; the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase fails for want of ATP so cells swell and K⁺ leaks out; lysosomal enzymes are released; endotoxin (lipid A) in septic shock triggers TNF-α, IL-1 and nitric oxide, causing vasodilation, capillary leak and DIC. Monitoring: blood lactate (the best prognostic indicator), base deficit, PCV/TP, glucose, electrolytes, creatinine, coagulation profile.

15 Biochemical basis of fluid therapy · Detoxification and xenobiotic metabolism

Fluid compartments. Total body water ≈ 60 % of body weight, split as ICF ≈ 40 % and ECF ≈ 20 % (interstitial 15 %, plasma 5 %). Na⁺ is the principal ECF cation and the determinant of ECF volume; K⁺ is the principal ICF cation. Water moves by osmosis; protein holds it in the vessel by oncotic pressure (Starling forces + the Donnan effect).

Prescribing in three parts: ① correct the existing deficit = BW(kg) × % dehydration × 10 = mL · ② replace ongoing losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, polyuria) · ③ supply maintenance ≈ 50–60 mL/kg/day. Then decide the route, the rate, and the potassium — never exceed 0.5 mEq K⁺/kg/hour.

Detoxification (biotransformation) converts lipid-soluble xenobiotics — drugs, plant toxins, pesticides, mycotoxins, food additives, industrial chemicals — into more water-soluble, less toxic, more readily excretable compounds. The liver is the chief site (smooth ER); also kidney, lung, intestinal mucosa and skin.

Phase I —
functionalisation
Oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, hydration and dehalogenation. Introduces or unmasks a polar group (−OH, −NH₂, −SH, −COOH). Dominated by the cytochrome P450 mixed-function oxidase system. Products may be more toxic than the parent — bioactivation.
Phase II —
conjugation
Attachment of a bulky polar endogenous molecule, always making the product less toxic and readily excreted: glucuronidation (UDP-glucuronyl transferase — the commonest), sulphation (PAPS), glutathione conjugation, acetylation, methylation (SAM), glycine conjugation (hippuric acid), and mercapturic-acid formation.

Cytochrome P450 — a superfamily of haem-thiolate monooxygenases named for the 450 nm absorbance of their CO complex. Reaction: RH + O₂ + NADPH + H⁺ → ROH + H₂O + NADP⁺ — one atom of oxygen enters the substrate, the other becomes water; the partner enzyme is NADPH–cytochrome P450 reductase. Besides xenobiotics, P450s make steroid hormones, bile acids, vitamin D and eicosanoids. They are strongly inducible (phenobarbitone, rifampicin, PCBs, some pesticides) and inhibitable (chloramphenicol, cimetidine, ketoconazole) — the mechanism of countless drug interactions and of altered withdrawal periods in food animals.

Species deficiencies — the most rewarding facts in this section: ① The cat has a deficient glucuronyl transferase, so it cannot glucuronidate efficiently — hence its notorious sensitivity to paracetamol (methaemoglobinaemia, Heinz bodies, facial oedema), aspirin, phenols and benzoic acid. ② The dog is a poor acetylator (deficient N-acetyltransferase) — sulphonamide handling differs. ③ The pig sulphates poorly. ④ Neonates of all species have immature phase-II systems. Antidote logic follows directly: N-acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione in paracetamol poisoning.
Trap — bioactivation. "Detoxification" does not always detoxify. Phase I can create a more dangerous metabolite: paracetamol → NAPQI (hepatotoxic and, in cats, met-Hb forming), parathion → paraoxon (a far stronger cholinesterase inhibitor), aflatoxin B₁ → its 8,9-epoxide (binds DNA, hepatocarcinogenic), ethylene glycol → glycolic and oxalic acid (metabolic acidosis and calcium-oxalate nephrosis, treated by blocking alcohol dehydrogenase with fomepizole or ethanol). Always name one such example when asked about biotransformation.
