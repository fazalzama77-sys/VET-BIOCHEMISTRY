01 Definition, properties & IUB classification

Enzymes are biocatalysts — proteins (except ribozymes, catalytic RNA) elaborated by living cells that accelerate a reaction by lowering its activation energy, without being consumed and without altering ΔG or the equilibrium constant. They only make equilibrium arrive sooner.

04 Enzyme–substrate complex, the modern active centre & specificity

E + S⇌ES→EP→E + P

Two models of the active site:

Lock & key
(Emil Fischer, 1894)
Active site is a rigid, pre-formed template exactly complementary to the substrate. Explains specificity well, but not the action of enzymes on several related substrates.
Induced fit
(Koshland, 1958)
The modern concept. The active site is flexible; the approaching substrate induces a conformational change that brings the catalytic groups into correct alignment — like a hand entering a glove. Explains allosteric regulation, competitive inhibition and non-productive binding.

Features of the active centre: ① a small region — only 3–5 % of the total enzyme volume; ② a crevice or cleft from which water is largely excluded; ③ built from amino-acid residues that are far apart in the primary sequence but brought together by folding; ④ has a substrate-binding (contact) site and a catalytic site; ⑤ binding is by weak, non-covalent forces — H-bond, ionic, hydrophobic, van der Waals; ⑥ residues commonly involved: serine, histidine, cysteine, aspartate, glutamate, lysine, tyrosine (the Ser–His–Asp catalytic triad of trypsin and chymotrypsin); ⑦ the shape complements the transition state, not the ground-state substrate.

05 Factors influencing enzyme action

① Substrate concentration. At low [S] the rate is first order (∝[S]); as [S] rises the curve flattens to a rectangular hyperbola and becomes zero order at saturation, when every active site is occupied → Vmax.

Michaelis–Menten v = Vmax[S] / (Km + [S])
Km = the substrate concentration at which v = ½Vmax (units of concentration, mol/L).
  • Low Km = high affinity for the substrate (hexokinase Km 0.1 mM) and vice versa (glucokinase 10 mM).
  • Km is a constant characteristic of the enzyme–substrate pair and is independent of enzyme concentration; Vmax is directly proportional to it.
  • Lineweaver–Burk (double reciprocal) plot linearises the curve: 1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax — y-intercept 1/Vmax, x-intercept −1/Km, slope Km/Vmax.

② Enzyme concentration. With substrate in excess the velocity is directly proportional to [E] — a straight line through the origin. This linearity is the basis of every enzyme assay in the clinical laboratory.

③ Temperature. Rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (Q₁₀ ≈ 2) up to the optimum (≈ 37–40 °C in mammals, 41–42 °C in birds), beyond which thermal denaturation causes a sharp fall. The curve is therefore bell-shaped, but asymmetrical. Enzymes are inactivated above ~60 °C; hence samples are refrigerated.

④ pH. Bell-shaped curve; extremes alter the ionisation of the active site and of the substrate, and finally denature the enzyme.

Pepsin 1.5–2.0 Acid phosphatase 5 Salivary amylase 6.8 Trypsin / lipase 8 ALP 9–10 Arginase 9.5

⑤ Others: time of incubation, accumulation of product (product inhibition), activators (metal ions, bile salts for lipase), inhibitors, light and UV/ionising radiation.

Enzyme units — learn all four definitions verbatim.

International Unit (IU)
The amount of enzyme that converts 1 µmol of substrate per minute under defined optimum conditions of temperature, pH and substrate concentration.
Katal (SI unit)
The amount of enzyme that converts 1 mole of substrate per second. 1 katal = 6 × 10⁷ IU; 1 IU = 16.67 nkat.
Specific activity
Units of enzyme activity per milligram of protein — the index of purity; it rises at every purification step.
Turnover number
(kcat, molecular activity)
The number of substrate molecules converted per molecule of enzyme (or per active site) per unit time when the enzyme is fully saturated. Highest known: carbonic anhydrase ≈ 600 000 per second.
Trap: specific activity measures purity; turnover number measures catalytic efficiency of a single molecule. Students routinely swap the two — a guaranteed one-mark loss.

07 Allosteric enzymes & the four modes of metabolic regulation

Allosteric ("other site") enzymes are the regulatory enzymes of a pathway. They are oligomeric, possess a catalytic site plus a separate allosteric site, and bind their effectors non-covalently and reversibly.

  • They give a sigmoid (S-shaped) v vs [S] curve, not a hyperbola — because subunits show co-operativity; they therefore do not obey Michaelis–Menten kinetics.
  • Positive effectors (activators) shift the curve left (↑affinity); negative effectors (inhibitors) shift it right.
  • Homotropic = the substrate is itself the effector (O₂ on haemoglobin); heterotropic = a different molecule (ATP on PFK-1).
  • Models: MWC concerted / symmetry model (all subunits flip T ⇌ R together) and KNF sequential model (one at a time).
  • Almost always the first committed, irreversible, rate-limiting step of the pathway, and usually subject to feedback (end-product) inhibition.
Learn these five allosteric enzymes with their effectors: PFK-1 (− ATP, citrate; + AMP, fructose-2,6-bisphosphate) · pyruvate carboxylase (+ acetyl-CoA) · glycogen phosphorylase (+ AMP in muscle; − ATP, G6P) · carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (+ N-acetylglutamate) · HMG-CoA reductase and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (+ citrate, − palmitoyl-CoA).

08 Enzymes & coenzymes of oxidation–reduction

Biological oxidation = removal of electrons (usually as hydrogen atoms) from a substrate, coupled to reduction of an acceptor. It proceeds in small steps down a redox-potential gradient so the energy is captured, not lost as a flash of heat.

10 Oxidative phosphorylation, uncouplers & the shuttles

Chemiosmotic theory — Peter Mitchell, 1961 (Nobel 1978). Electron flow through Complexes I, III and IV pumps H⁺ from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating a proton-motive force made of a pH gradient (ΔpH) plus a membrane potential (Δψ). Protons re-enter only through the F₀ channel of ATP synthase (Complex V), and this flow drives rotational catalysis in F₁ (Boyer's binding-change mechanism) which releases ATP.

  • Prerequisite: an intact, impermeable inner mitochondrial membrane. Any leak abolishes ATP synthesis while electron flow continues.
  • Respiratory control: the rate of the chain is governed by the availability of ADP — the cell makes ATP only as fast as it spends it.
  • P : O ratio = molecules of ATP formed per atom of oxygen reduced: NADH = 2.5 (classically 3), FADH₂ = 1.5 (classically 2).

Uncouplers carry H⁺ back across the membrane, short-circuiting the gradient. Electron transport continues (even accelerates) but ATP is not made — the energy appears as heat. Signs: hyperthermia, tachypnoea, weight loss, no ATP gain.

2,4-Dinitrophenol, dinitro-o-cresol CCCP, FCCP Salicylate in overdose Unconjugated bilirubin Excess thyroxine, free fatty acids Thermogenin (UCP-1) — physiological
Veterinary gold: brown adipose tissue of the newborn lamb, piglet, kid and kitten is packed with UCP-1/thermogenin, a deliberate, hormone-controlled uncoupler that generates heat instead of ATP — non-shivering thermogenesis. Its failure or absence explains neonatal hypothermia and much of the "weak-neonate" complex.
Trap: distinguish sharply — an inhibitor stops electron flow and ATP synthesis and oxygen consumption falls; an uncoupler lets electron flow and O₂ consumption increase while ATP falls; oligomycin blocks the ATP synthase itself, so both stop — and adding an uncoupler restores respiration after oligomycin. That last sentence is a favourite viva question.

Shuttles. The inner membrane is impermeable to NADH, so cytosolic NADH from glycolysis must be transferred indirectly — and the choice of shuttle decides the final ATP count for glucose.

2 × G-3-PGAPDH  +2 NADH→2 × 1,3-BPG+2 ATP (SLP)→3-PG → 2-PGenolase→2 × PEPpyruvate kinase +2 ATP (SLP)→2 × Pyruvate
  • Three irreversible, regulatory steps: hexokinase/glucokinase, PFK-1 (the key rate-limiting enzyme), pyruvate kinase. These are exactly the three bypassed in gluconeogenesis.
  • Balance sheet: 2 ATP invested, 4 ATP generated by substrate-level phosphorylation → net 2 ATP + 2 NADH per glucose. Anaerobically the 2 NADH are spent reducing pyruvate to lactate (LDH) to regenerate NAD⁺, so the net stays 2 ATP — the only ATP source for the RBC (no mitochondria), working muscle, cornea, renal medulla and rapidly growing tissue.
  • Hexokinase vs glucokinase: hexokinase is in all tissues, Km 0.1 mM, inhibited by G-6-P; glucokinase is in liver and β-cell, Km ≈ 10 mM, not inhibited by G-6-P and induced by insulin — it only works after a meal, letting the liver take up glucose only when blood glucose is high.
  • Rapoport–Luebering shunt in the RBC diverts 1,3-BPG to 2,3-BPG, which lowers haemoglobin's O₂ affinity and improves tissue delivery — at the cost of one ATP.
Practical link you will be asked: sodium fluoride inhibits enolase, so NaF-containing (grey-top) tubes are used for blood glucose collection — without it, erythrocyte glycolysis destroys ~10 % of the glucose per hour and gives a falsely low result. Also: iodoacetate blocks GAPDH, arsenate uncouples at GAPDH (glycolysis runs but makes no ATP).

Link reaction — pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (mitochondrial matrix): pyruvate + CoA + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH. A multienzyme complex of 3 enzymes needing 5 coenzymes: TPP, Lipoamide, CoA, FAD, NAD⁺. Irreversible — this single fact is why fatty acids can never be converted to glucose in animals. Inhibited by arsenite and by mercury (bind lipoamide −SH); switched off by phosphorylation (PDH kinase, activated by ATP/NADH/acetyl-CoA) and on by dephosphorylation (PDH phosphatase, Ca²⁺, insulin).

PDH coenzymes — "Tender Loving Care For Nancy": TPP, Lipoamide, CoA, FAD, NAD⁺. The α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex uses exactly the same five.

13 HMP shunt (pentose phosphate pathway)

Site: cytosol of liver, adipose tissue, lactating mammary gland, adrenal cortex, gonads, RBC and leucocytes — all tissues that need NADPH. Also called the Warburg–Dickens–Horecker or hexose monophosphate pathway. It produces no ATP and consumes none.

G-6-PG-6-P dehydrogenase · rate-limiting · NADPH #1→6-phosphogluconate6-PG DH · NADPH #2 + CO₂→Ribulose-5-P
Ribulose-5-P→Ribose-5-Pnon-oxidative, reversible: transketolase (TPP) & transaldolase→F-6-P + G-3-P back to glycolysis
  • Two products, both irreplaceable: NADPH (2 per G-6-P; 12 per glucose fully cycled) and ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotides, ATP, NAD, FAD, CoA.
  • What NADPH is spent on: ① fatty acid, cholesterol and steroid-hormone synthesis — hence the pathway's intense activity in adipose tissue, mammary gland and adrenal cortex; ② reduction of glutathione (glutathione reductase) → GSH, the RBC's shield against oxidant damage; ③ the respiratory burst of neutrophils via NADPH oxidase; ④ cytochrome P450 hydroxylations in detoxification; ⑤ reduction of folate and of methaemoglobin.
Clinical anchor: when the NADPH–glutathione shield is overwhelmed, haemoglobin is oxidised → Heinz-body haemolytic anaemia and methaemoglobinaemia. Classic Indian-practice causes: onion and Brassica/kale poisoning in cattle and dogs, paracetamol in the cat, red maple in the horse, and phenothiazine.

14 Gluconeogenesis · Cori cycle · glycogen metabolism · bioenergetics

Gluconeogenesis — synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources. Site: liver (≈90 %) and renal cortex (≈10 %); spans cytosol, mitochondrion and endoplasmic reticulum. Substrates: lactate, glycerol, propionate (the ruminant's chief one), and glucogenic amino acids — alanine above all.

Cost: 6 high-energy phosphates (4 ATP + 2 GTP) plus 2 NADH per glucose — gluconeogenesis is expensive, which is why the liver will not run it and glycolysis at the same time (reciprocal regulation).
Golden rule: acetyl-CoA (and therefore even-chain fatty acids) can never be converted to glucose — the PDH step is irreversible. Only glycerol and odd-chain/propionate carbons from fat are glucogenic.

Cori cycle (lactic acid cycle). Muscle glycolysis under anaerobic work → lactate → blood → liver → gluconeogenesis → glucose → back to muscle. It shifts the metabolic burden of the muscle to the liver and clears lactic acid, at a net cost of 4 ATP to the liver for every 2 recovered by muscle.

Muscle glucose → lactate→blood→Liver lactate → glucose→blood → muscle

The parallel glucose–alanine (Cahill) cycle carries both the carbon and the amino nitrogen of muscle protein to the liver, where alanine is transaminated to pyruvate for gluconeogenesis and its nitrogen enters the urea cycle.

Glycogenesis. Glucose → G-6-P → G-1-P (phosphoglucomutase) → UDP-glucose (uses UTP) → glycogen. Glycogen synthase makes the α1→4 chain on the primer protein glycogenin; branching enzyme (amylo-4,6-transglucosidase) transfers blocks of ~7 residues to make α1→6 branches. Activated by insulin (dephosphorylation).

Glycogenolysis. Glycogen phosphorylase (rate-limiting, needs PLP) carries out phosphorolysis, releasing G-1-P; the debranching enzyme (transferase + α1,6-glucosidase) releases the ~8 % of residues at branch points as free glucose. Driven by glucagon and adrenaline via cAMP → protein kinase A, which simultaneously switches synthase off — one signal, opposite effects, no futile cycle.

Ruminant special (worth a whole long answer): almost no glucose is absorbed from the gut, so the ruminant runs gluconeogenesis continuously, all day, mainly from propionate: propionate → propionyl-CoA → methylmalonyl-CoA →(B₁₂, mutase)→ succinyl-CoA → OAA → glucose. Cobalt/vitamin B₁₂ deficiency blocks this step and causes wasting; and any surge in demand (peak lactation, twin pregnancy) that outruns propionate supply produces ketosis and pregnancy toxaemia — the whole of Unit 3's energy section rests on this paragraph.

Four repeating steps, each cycle shortening the chain by two carbons — mnemonic "Ox–Hy–Ox–Th":

  1. Oxidation — acyl-CoA dehydrogenase → FADH₂
  2. Hydration — enoyl-CoA hydratase
  3. Oxidation — β-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase → NADH
  4. Thiolysis — thiolase → acetyl-CoA + acyl-CoA (n−2)
Palmitate (C16) bioenergetics — memorise the arithmetic: 7 cycles → 8 acetyl-CoA + 7 FADH₂ + 7 NADH.
(8 × 10) + (7 × 1.5) + (7 × 2.5) = 80 + 10.5 + 17.5 = 108 − 2 (activation) = 106 ATP  (classical figures: 129 − 2 = 129).
Cycles = (n/2) − 1; acetyl-CoA = n/2.
  • Carnitine is synthesised from lysine + methionine and is the obligatory ferry for long-chain fatty acids; short and medium chains enter freely. Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I — the elegant switch that stops the cell burning and building fat at once.
  • Odd-chain fatty acids end in propionyl-CoA → methylmalonyl-CoA →(B₁₂)→ succinyl-CoA — the only part of a fatty acid that is glucogenic. Unsaturated fatty acids need an extra isomerase and reductase. Very-long-chain fatty acids are shortened in peroxisomes; α-oxidation handles branched phytanic acid; ω-oxidation is a minor microsomal route.

16 Ketone-body formation & fatty acid biosynthesis

Ketogenesis — liver mitochondria only, by the HMG-CoA pathway.

2 Acetyl-CoA→Acetoacetyl-CoAHMG-CoA synthase — rate-limiting→HMG-CoAHMG-CoA lyase→Acetoacetate
AcetoacetateBHB dehydrogenase, NADH→β-hydroxybutyrate — the major one·spontaneous decarboxylation→Acetone — exhaled, "sweet breath"
  • The liver makes ketones but cannot use them — it lacks thiophorase (succinyl-CoA : acetoacetate CoA transferase). Heart, skeletal muscle, renal cortex and, in prolonged starvation, the brain oxidise them; ketone bodies are water-soluble, need no carrier and spare glucose.
  • Trigger in every species: low insulin / high glucagon → unrestrained lipolysis → acetyl-CoA floods the liver, while oxaloacetate is drained away into gluconeogenesis, so acetyl-CoA cannot enter the TCA cycle. Hence the classical statement — "fat burns in the flame of carbohydrate."
  • Causes: starvation, high-fat/low-carbohydrate diet, diabetes mellitus, bovine ketosis of early lactation, pregnancy toxaemia of the ewe and doe.
Ruminant twist: β-hydroxybutyrate is a normal ruminant metabolite — the rumen epithelium converts absorbed butyrate to BHB during every meal. Ketosis is therefore a matter of degree, and BHB is the field test: <1.0 mmol/L normal · 1.2–1.4 subclinical · >2.6–3.0 clinical ketosis.

Fatty acid biosynthesis (de novo lipogenesis) — cytosol. Acetyl-CoA is exported from the mitochondrion as citrate (ATP-citrate lyase). Acetyl-CoA carboxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme: it needs biotin, CO₂ and ATP, is activated by citrate and insulin and inhibited by palmitoyl-CoA, glucagon and adrenaline, and it makes malonyl-CoA. Fatty acid synthase, a multienzyme dimer carrying ACP (phosphopantetheine, from pantothenic acid), then repeats condensation → reduction → dehydration → reduction and stops at palmitate (C16). Per palmitate: 8 acetyl-CoA, 7 ATP, 14 NADPH (from the HMP shunt and malic enzyme). Elongation and desaturation (Δ9, Δ6, Δ5 only) follow in the ER.

Species trap: in ruminants the principal site of lipogenesis is adipose tissue and the mammary gland, not the liver, and the starting material is acetate absorbed from the rumen, not citrate-derived acetyl-CoA. This is why a low-fibre ration (less acetate) causes milk-fat depression.

17 Protein metabolism — biosynthesis, degradation and the fate of the amino group

Amino acid pool & nitrogen balance. Dietary protein, tissue protein turnover and de-novo synthesis feed a common pool (≈100 g in an adult) drawn on for protein synthesis, hormones, purines, haem, creatine, and for energy. Positive balance = growth, pregnancy, lactation, convalescence; negative balance = starvation, fever, trauma, parasitism, protein-deficient ration.

Degradation follows two routes: the ATP-dependent ubiquitin–proteasome system in the cytosol for short-lived and abnormal proteins (tagged by polyubiquitin), and lysosomal cathepsins for extracellular and membrane proteins. Biosynthesis is ribosomal translation of mRNA (see Sheet 6).

Transamination
Reversible transfer of an α-amino group to an α-keto acid. Coenzyme pyridoxal phosphate (B₆). ALT: alanine + α-KG ⇌ pyruvate + glutamate. AST: aspartate + α-KG ⇌ OAA + glutamate. Every amino acid except lysine, threonine, proline and hydroxyproline undergoes it. Its purpose is to funnel all nitrogen into glutamate. Leakage of ALT/AST into serum is the basis of liver and muscle profiling in Unit 3.
Deamination
Oxidative — glutamate dehydrogenase (mitochondrial, NAD(P)⁺, reversible, allosterically inhibited by ATP/GTP and activated by ADP/GDP) is the chief route and the only one that liberates ammonia rapidly; L- and D-amino acid oxidases (FMN/FAD) are minor and give H₂O₂. Non-oxidative — serine/threonine dehydratase, cysteine desulphydrase, histidase.
Transdeamination
Transamination + oxidative deamination coupled — the combined route by which the nitrogen of almost all amino acids finally reaches ammonia.

Decarboxylation (PLP-dependent) removes −COOH as CO₂ and yields the biogenic amines — small marks, frequently asked:

Ammonia transport. Free NH₃ is intensely neurotoxic (it drains α-ketoglutarate from the brain's TCA cycle and swells astrocytes as glutamine accumulates). It travels in blood in two safe forms: glutamine (glutamine synthetase, ATP; released by glutaminase in liver and kidney) — the principal carrier — and alanine from muscle by the glucose–alanine cycle.

Cost: 4 ATP equivalents (3 ATP molecules) per urea. One nitrogen from free ammonia, one from aspartate; the carbon from CO₂.
Clinical payoffs: ① Portosystemic shunt in the dog — high blood ammonia, low BUN, ammonium biurate crystals, hepatic encephalopathy. ② Urea poisoning in ruminants — rumen urease liberates NH₃ faster than the liver can clear it; rumen pH rises, absorption accelerates; treat with cold water + 5 % acetic acid (vinegar). ③ Nitrogen recycling — urea returns to the rumen in saliva and across the wall, where microbes reuse it, so ruminants thrive on non-protein nitrogen. ④ Ureotelic (mammals) vs uricotelic (birds, reptiles) vs ammonotelic (fish).

18 Purine & pyrimidine metabolism

Purine —
de novo
Built on the ribose: R-5-P →(PRPP synthetase)→ PRPP →(glutamine-PRPP amidotransferase — committed, rate-limiting step, feedback-inhibited by AMP, GMP and IMP)→ 11 steps → IMP → AMP (needs GTP + aspartate) or GMP (needs ATP + glutamine). The reciprocal ATP/GTP requirement keeps the two pools balanced. Atoms come from glycine, glutamine, aspartate, CO₂ and formyl-THF.
Purine —
salvage
HGPRT (hypoxanthine, guanine) and APRT (adenine) reattach free bases to PRPP — energetically far cheaper, and vital in brain and bone marrow, which cannot synthesise de novo.
Purine —
catabolism
AMP/GMP → inosine/guanosine → hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid by xanthine oxidase (a molybdenum flavoprotein of liver and intestinal mucosa). Most mammals then convert uric acid to soluble allantoin by uricase.
Pyrimidine
The ring is made first and attached to PRPP afterwards. CPS-II (cytosolic, uses glutamine, needs no N-acetylglutamate) is rate-limiting → carbamoyl aspartate → dihydroorotate → orotate → OMP → UMP → UTP → CTP. dTMP comes from dUMP by thymidylate synthase using N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF.
Deoxy-
ribonucleotides
Ribonucleotide reductase reduces NDP → dNDP using thioredoxin; allosterically regulated; inhibited by hydroxyurea.
The three points that always appear: ① Birds and reptiles are uricotelic — they lack uricase and excrete uric acid as a paste; overload gives avian visceral and articular gout (high-protein ration, renal damage, water deprivation). ② The Dalmatian dog handles urate abnormally and forms urate uroliths. ③ Allopurinol is a suicide inhibitor of xanthine oxidase; 5-fluorouracil blocks thymidylate synthase and methotrexate blocks dihydrofolate reductase — the classical antimetabolites. Pyrimidine catabolism, by contrast, gives soluble β-alanine and β-aminoisobutyrate, so pyrimidines never form stones.

19 DNA & RNA biosynthesis and its regulation

Replication is semi-conservative (Meselson–Stahl), bidirectional from an origin, and every polymerase works only 5′→3′.

Helicase unwinds→SSB + topoisomerase/gyrase→Primase lays RNA primer→DNA pol III extends→Pol I removes primer→Ligase seals
  • Leading strand continuous; lagging strand discontinuous as Okazaki fragments. Fidelity comes from the 3′→5′ exonuclease proofreading of the polymerase. Eukaryotic polymerases: α (primase), δ and ε (elongation), γ (mitochondrial); telomerase extends chromosome ends.
  • Transcription: DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, needs no primer, reads the template (antisense) strand 3′→5′ and builds RNA 5′→3′. Prokaryotic core α₂ββ′ω + sigma factor for promoter recognition (Pribnow box TATAAT at −10), rho factor for termination. Eukaryotes: RNA pol I → rRNA, pol II → mRNA, pol III → tRNA & 5S; TATA (Hogness) box.
  • Post-transcriptional processing of eukaryotic mRNA: 5′ capping with 7-methylguanosine, 3′ polyadenylation, and splicing out of introns by the spliceosome.
  • Inhibitors worth naming: rifampicin → bacterial RNA polymerase; actinomycin D → intercalates DNA; α-amanitin → RNA pol II (Amanita mushroom poisoning); fluoroquinolones → DNA gyrase; acyclovir/AZT → nucleoside analogues.
  • Regulation — Jacob & Monod operon model. lac operon (inducible): allolactose inactivates the repressor, transcription begins; superimposed catabolite repression by glucose via cAMP–CAP. trp operon (repressible): tryptophan acts as co-repressor and switches the operon off. In eukaryotes: promoters and enhancers, transcription factors, steroid-hormone response elements, DNA methylation, histone acetylation and miRNA.
  • Translation: the genetic code is a triplet, degenerate, non-overlapping, comma-less and near-universal; AUG starts, UAA/UAG/UGA stop; wobble at the third base. Antibiotics: streptomycin & aminoglycosides (30 S, misreading), tetracycline (30 S A-site), chloramphenicol (50 S peptidyl transferase), macrolides (50 S translocation).

20 Regulation & integration of metabolism

Three junction molecules decide the fate of everything: glucose-6-phosphate (glycogen / glycolysis / HMP shunt), pyruvate (lactate / acetyl-CoA / OAA / alanine) and acetyl-CoA — the true crossroads, from which carbon can go to TCA, fatty acids, cholesterol or ketone bodies but never back to glucose.

BrainObligate glucose user; switches to ketone bodies only after days of starvation; no fuel stores

Fed state (insulin dominant): glucose uptake via GLUT-4, glycogenesis, lipogenesis, protein synthesis; glycolysis and the HMP shunt run; PDH is dephosphorylated and active.

Fasting and starvation (glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, GH): a strict sequence — ① hepatic glycogenolysis (first 12–24 h) → ② gluconeogenesis from amino acids, lactate and glycerol → ③ lipolysis and ketogenesis, which spare glucose for the brain → ④ protein catabolism as the last resort. Muscle switches to fatty acids and ketones within days.

Ruminant integration — write this as your conclusion in any long answer: the ruminant absorbs virtually no glucose. Instead it absorbs VFAs: acetate (≈65 %) → lipogenesis and milk fat; propionate (≈20 %) → the sole major gluconeogenic precursor; butyrate (≈15 %) → converted to β-hydroxybutyrate by the rumen wall. Therefore the ruminant is permanently gluconeogenic, permanently mildly ketogenic, and any negative energy balance — peak lactation, twin pregnancy, transport, anorexia — tips it straight into ketosis or pregnancy toxaemia. Everything in Unit 3's energy-disorder section is this paragraph applied clinically.
Trap: the ruminant's low blood glucose (45–60 mg/dL) is normal, not hypoglycaemia; and a "normal" glucose in a ketotic cow does not exclude ketosis — BHB, not glucose, is the diagnostic analyte.
